In this article I'm not going to talk about macOS, because it's a very good
OS and it's close enough to Linux anyway. I want to tell you about my move
from Windows to Linux (Fedora Linux) as a developer who works with coding
agents: why AI agents are so much better on Linux than on Windows, why I
made the switch, what I got out of it, and how the two compare technically
and in day-to-day use.

The first reason I ended up on Linux is speed. PowerShell is, in my
opinion, the worst-designed terminal engine out there, at least for AI and
for a developer in 2026. Every command is slow. I'll come back to this
later in the article, because it's the clearest example of just how
obsolete Windows' approach has become next to Linux.

The second reason is that Windows is not a fast, lightweight OS. You'll
tell me Linux is heavy too, and you're right about disk footprint, but what
I mean by lightweight is something else. I'll explain further down.

And finally, Windows is a paid, locked-down OS. You can do what Microsoft
allows. On Linux you can do what you can think of: everything is open,
configurable, customizable, and at a depth that's hard to overstate.
Windows is proprietary. Linux lets you make the OS yours.

## The terminal, the example that sums up everything else

I promised I'd come back to PowerShell. Here's why it's the perfect example.

On my Fedora machine, I timed shell startup. `bash -c 'exit'`, repeated two
hundred times: 1.05 milliseconds per launch. `zsh`: 1.21 ms. A `fork` +
`exec` of a trivial binary: 0.60 ms. A `git status --porcelain`: 2 ms on a
small repo, 6.8 ms on a repo with seven hundred files.

On the PowerShell side, the numbers reported in the project's official
issue (PowerShell/PowerShell#6443) are 233.7 ms for `powershell.exe` and
540.0 ms for `pwsh`, the Core version. That's not my machine, so don't take
the comparison literally. But we're not talking about a 20% gap here, we're
talking about two orders of magnitude. And the problem has been known for
years: another issue, #17734 "Slow startup of Powershell Core", was opened
and then closed without anything fundamental changing. One optimization
attempt did land on Microsoft's side, PR #8341 "Improve powershell startup
time", merged in December 2018: it brought startup down from 540.5 ms to
408.3 ms, a 24.5% improvement. Still two orders of magnitude away from a
POSIX shell.

Now, the fundamentals. This isn't laziness, it's architectural.

Windows has no `fork()`. It only has `CreateProcess`, a single call that
builds the entire process in one shot. The Git for Windows project
documents this explicitly: POSIX tools that rely on `fork` + `exec` have to
go through a significantly slower emulation layer. On Linux, `fork()`
copies nothing. It marks the pages copy-on-write and returns. That's why I
measure 0.60 ms where Windows has to load the loader, resolve DLLs, spin up
the CLR for PowerShell, and notify every component listening for process
creation along the way.

An independent benchmark on identical hardware (Bits'n'Bites, "Benchmarking
OS primitives") measured, back in June 2017, Linux more than 20 times
faster than Windows at launching a full program, roughly 3 times faster at
thread creation, and up to a thousand times faster at file creation between
the best Linux configuration and the worst Windows one, the latter running
a third-party antivirus. The benchmark is nine years old, so take it for
what it is: an order of magnitude, not a measurement of today. The damning
detail is elsewhere, in the methodology: to get his best Windows numbers,
the author had to disable Windows Defender and search indexing.

That detail isn't a footnote, it's the heart of the problem. On Windows,
every process launched, every file opened, every write goes through a stack
of filter drivers: real-time Defender, AMSI, indexing, plus whatever your
company has piled on top. Microsoft admits it in its own way: they ship a
"Performance analyzer for Microsoft Defender Antivirus" whose entire
purpose is to tell you which files and which processes consume the most
scan time. You don't ship a diagnostic tool for a cost that doesn't exist.

Bruce Dawson, a Google engineer and Microsoft alumnus, documented an even
more telling case: a quadratic algorithm inside `CreateProcess` (updating
the Control Flow Guard bitmap) that made performance collapse depending on
address space fragmentation. In his repro case, disabling CFG made
`CreateProcess` about twenty times faster.

And if you're thinking "sure, but I use WSL", Microsoft itself has the
answer. The official documentation for setting up a dev environment on WSL
recommends storing your files in the Linux file system, because
cross-access through `/mnt/*` "can significantly slow performance".
Independent measurements from vxlabs put numbers on it: host files are
roughly 5 times slower to process under WSL 2 than under WSL 1, a `yarn
build` is 5.8 times slower, and a `du` walking a hundred thousand files,
which is pure metadata access, ranges from 14 to 31 times slower. The
reason is that `/mnt/c` speaks the 9p protocol, a network file system
protocol, across the VM/host boundary. Every `open`, every `stat` is a
round trip. A virtiofs transport has since appeared, enabled through
`.wslconfig`, but 9p is still the default. So your choice is to work in
NTFS and pay the network tax, or work in ext4 and not really be on Windows
anymore.

## Why coding agents are better on Linux

This is where everything above stops being a comfort question and becomes a
measurable productivity question.

A coding agent doesn't do one big operation, it does hundreds of small
ones. It lists files, it greps, it runs a test, it reads the output, it
runs it again. The agent loop is a process-creation machine. That's exactly
the worst case for Windows and the best case for a POSIX kernel.

Do the math yourself. Two hundred commands in a session, which is a normal
session, not an extreme one. At 400 ms of shell startup, you lose 80
seconds of pure waiting. At 1 ms, you lose 0.2 seconds. That latency
doesn't show up in a benchmark, it shows up in the fact that the agent
looks like it's thinking when it's really just waiting on its shell.

Add POSIX chaining. `rg --files-with-matches 'pattern' | xargs sed -i` is
one line. The agent writes it in a single pass, the shell understands it,
done. The PowerShell equivalent exists, but it's more verbose, objects
replace text, and models have seen vastly more POSIX shell than PowerShell
in training. An agent is best where it has read the most.

But the real argument, the one that settled it for me, is sandboxing.

In May 2026, OpenAI published an engineering post about building their
Codex sandbox for Windows. The sentence worth remembering is theirs, not
mine: "Windows does not provide a single primitive that cleanly maps to a
safe execution environment for agentic workloads." The post explains that
when the author joined the team, Codex for Windows simply had no sandbox
implementation at all: Windows users had to choose between approving nearly
every command, or enabling a "Full Access" mode with no restrictions
whatsoever. The final solution rests on restricted tokens, synthetic SIDs,
dedicated sandbox users, and firewall rules, an architecture built entirely
by hand.

On Linux, there's nothing to build. Codex today relies on bubblewrap, which
stacks namespaces, `PR_SET_NO_NEW_PRIVS`, and a seccomp filter. Landlock, a
kernel security module, remains available as a legacy fallback path. On
macOS, it's Seatbelt. In both cases, these are primitives already present
in the system, directly usable.

I checked on my own machine: `CONFIG_SECURITY_LANDLOCK=y`,
`CONFIG_SECCOMP=y`, `CONFIG_USER_NS=y`, bubblewrap 0.11 installed, SELinux
in Enforcing mode, podman 5.8.4. It's all there by default, nothing to
install.

On the Claude Code side, Anthropic's documentation is just as blunt: "The
sandbox is built into Claude Code and runs on macOS, Linux, and WSL2.
Native Windows is not supported." The sandbox runs on macOS, Linux, and WSL
2, not on native Windows and not on WSL 1. On native Windows, Claude Code
falls back to Git Bash if Git for Windows is installed, and otherwise runs
commands through its PowerShell tool.

Translate that into practice. On Linux, I can let an agent work with broad
permissions because the kernel, not the agent, guarantees it won't step
outside its perimeter. On native Windows, either I approve every command
and become the bottleneck again, or I hand over full power and trust.
Neither option is acceptable when you're working seriously with agents.

One last point: containers. On Linux, a container is namespaces and
cgroups, two kernel features you're already running. There's no virtual
machine. On Windows, Docker Desktop runs a Linux VM, with its reserved
memory, its own file system, and the boundary crossing mentioned above the
moment you mount a volume from the host. Your devcontainers aren't less
convenient on Windows, they're just heavier, every single run.

## What I mean by "lightweight"

I told you I wasn't talking about disk footprint. My Fedora install takes
up hundreds of gigabytes and counts nearly three thousand packages. On that
front, I have nothing to sell you.

What I call lightweight is the amount of stuff running that I never asked
for.

On my machine, 48 systemd services are running. I can name every one of
them, I can disable every one of them, and the OS will keep working while
letting me live with the consequences. Nothing is watching my activity to
report it elsewhere. Nothing is reserving CPU for a feature I don't use.

On Windows 11, telemetry has three levels documented by Microsoft: Off,
Required, and Optional. The Required level, the one that sends device
information, app compatibility data, and Store data, cannot be fully
disabled on Home or Pro editions: full opt-out is reserved for Windows
Server, Enterprise, and Education. That's written in their own
documentation. The Start menu has a "Recommended" section that surfaces
promoted Store apps, picked by Microsoft from a limited set of developers,
disableable, but on by default. Recall, on Copilot+ PCs, takes snapshots of
your screen, with local processing and TPM encryption, opt-in, but present.

None of these things is a scandal on its own. The point is elsewhere: these
are decisions someone else made for me, on my machine, and that I can only
soften, never remove.

I have to be honest about one thing: I couldn't find a recent, independent,
reproducible benchmark comparing idle memory usage between Windows 11 and
Fedora Workstation on identical hardware. The numbers floating around come
from forums and unverifiable screenshots. So I'm not going to quote any.
The difference I see day to day is real to me, but I have no solid source
to put in front of you, and a feeling isn't a measurement.

## Paid and frozen, versus free and malleable

Windows 11 Home costs $139.99 as a retail license, Pro $199.99. Fedora
Workstation costs nothing, and the current release as I write this, Fedora
Linux 44, shipped on April 28, 2026 with GNOME 50. The X.org session had
already disappeared in Fedora 43: on my machine only Xwayland is left.

But the purchase price isn't the real issue. The real issue is what
happened on October 14, 2025: the end of support for Windows 10. To keep
receiving security updates, consumers had to go through the ESU program,
free by syncing their settings via Windows Backup, or for 1,000 Microsoft
Rewards points, or for $30. Microsoft extended the consumer track in June
2026: it now runs through October 12, 2027. For businesses, it's $61 per
seat the first year, then $122, then $244, an annual doubling that isn't a
pricing mistake but an eviction strategy.

And to move to Windows 11 you need TPM 2.0, Secure Boot enabled, UEFI, and
a CPU on an approved compatibility list. Perfectly functional machines were
declared ineligible.

That's where the difference in kind shows up. On Windows, you're a tenant.
You do what the vendor allows, for as long as the vendor decides, on the
hardware the vendor approves.

On Fedora, my setup looks like me because I built it. My window manager, my
shortcuts, my dotfiles versioned in a Git repo, my shell, my services. If a
machine dies, I reinstall and clone. Nothing is buried in a registry or in
a checkbox on a settings panel that will have moved by the next major
update. It's all text, all versionable, all reproducible.

And that property has a side effect I hadn't anticipated: it's also what
makes an agent effective. An agent knows how to read and edit a text config
file. It doesn't know how to click in a GUI. A system where everything is
text is a system an AI can actually administer.

## What the numbers say

I'm not going to tell you Linux won the desktop. According to StatCounter,
in July 2026 the worldwide desktop split was still Windows at 71.18%, macOS
at 7.61%, and Linux at 7.53%. Watch out for the trap I almost fell into:
StatCounter counts a separate "OS X" line at 12.21%, which puts Apple's
total near 20%. No, Linux did not pass macOS, it's still behind even on the
"macOS" line alone. And Windows still holds more than seven machines out of
ten.

While we're here, if you saw articles in July 2026 announcing Windows below
60% and Linux around 20%, forget them. Here's what actually happened: in
June 2026, StatCounter showed Windows at 56.55% and an "Unknown" category
at 21.45%, inflated by misclassified user agents. Linux was at 4.39%. Some
sites attributed the "Unknown" block to Linux. After correction, Windows
was back around 72%.

On Steam, Linux hit a record 5.33% in March 2026, then fell back to 4.52%
in April, 3.99% in May, 3.69% in June, and climbed back to 4.01% in July.
The March peak looks more like a survey anomaly than a shift. The
underlying trend is still clear: we came from 3.05% in October 2025.

Among developers, the Stack Overflow 2025 survey, published in late July
2025, gives Windows 56.7% for personal use and 49.5% for professional use,
macOS around 32.9% professionally, Ubuntu at 27.8% personal and 27.7%
professional, and WSL at 15.9% personal and 16.8% professional. The 2026
edition opened on June 23, 2026, but the results aren't out yet as I write
this.

What I read in these numbers isn't a shift. It's that Linux is already the
majority where code runs, and the minority where code is typed. The WSL
percentage is the most revealing of all: these are developers who have
already decided they want a Linux kernel, but haven't taken the step of
leaving Windows. I was exactly there for years.

## What it cost me

An article like this that only covered the wins would be advertising, not a
field report.

Plenty of apps don't exist on Linux, and the alternatives often aren't
equivalent. That's a fact. Some multiplayer games with kernel-level
anticheat will never run, no matter how far Proton comes. Microsoft Office
is web-only. On NVIDIA cards, Wayland was painful for a long time. It's
much better today, but it isn't fully smooth sailing yet. And there's the
learning curve, which is real: for the first few weeks you'll be less
productive, because you'll be looking up how to do things you used to do
without thinking.

If your job depends on any of those points, don't migrate. The right call
depends on what you run, not on ideology.

But know this: today, with AI agents, Linux has never been this accessible.
I'm an *AI maximalist*. I give my agents full access to my OS, and I
wouldn't go back to Windows for anything because of that. It's a kind of
comfort you only understand once you've made the jump.

And no, that doesn't contradict what I wrote above about Codex's "Full
Access" mode on Windows. Giving an agent full power isn't a question of
trust, it's a question of recovery cost. On my machine, the file system is
btrfs with snapper, SELinux runs in Enforcing mode, my configs are
versioned in Git, and my packages reinstall with one command. The worst
case costs me a rollback and a coffee. On a Windows box whose state lives
in a registry and in folders I can neither read nor diff, that same worst
case costs me a full reinstall. It isn't the same full access.

## Conclusion

I didn't switch to Linux out of activism, or because Windows is badly
built. I switched because the way I work changed.

Back when I typed every command myself, 400 ms of shell startup was
invisible: I was thinking during that time. Now that agents run the
commands, hundreds per session, those 400 ms have become the limiting
factor. And when I had to give them enough freedom to be useful, I needed a
kernel capable of containing them. On Linux, that's a system call. On
Windows, OpenAI had to spend months building it, and they say so in writing.

Windows didn't get worse. Developer work moved toward a model that Linux
was already serving, all along, without ever planning for it.
