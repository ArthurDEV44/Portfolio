We tend to believe that running a model at the top of its capabilities
guarantees the best result. It does not, and it often does the opposite. I am
writing this after several years of running coding agents in production, on
real projects. I take Opus 5 as the reference but this applies to every model
(gpt-5.6-sol, terra, luna, grok-4.5, Kimi K3, and the rest).

The reflex is always the same when you discover effort levels: "I need to set
<span className="rainbow-text">max</span> to get the best output." That reflex
is exactly what costs you, and not only in tokens.

## What effort actually controls

On Claude Code the levels are `low`, `medium`, `high`, `xhigh` and `max`. One
clarification first, because the confusion is everywhere:
<span className="shimmer-text">ultracode</span> is not an effort level. It is a
multi-agent orchestration keyword, and it belongs to a different mechanism.
Putting them in the same list muddles everything that follows.

What effort controls is thinking depth and overall token spend. A low effort
produces fewer reasoning turns, less preamble, and above all fewer, more
consolidated tool calls. A high effort does the opposite: the model explores
more before answering.

That has a consequence Anthropic's documentation states plainly: `max` can
deliver gains on some use cases, but it shows diminishing returns from the
increased token spend, and it is prone to overthinking. `max` is not a better
`xhigh`, it is a costlier one, with a risk of circling on tasks that do not
deserve it.

The corollary is more surprising still. On Opus 5 the official recommendation
is to start at `xhigh` for coding and agentic work, `high` for the rest, then
sweep downward, because `low` and `medium` hold up far better than expected on
this model. Step down one level as long as the output holds.

One nuance before going further. Saying that `low` holds up is not saying that
every level is equivalent. On genuinely hard tasks the gap is real. What is
documented is that the low levels hold up better than their reputation, not
that they match the high ones.

## Scientists, engineers, workers

Take a complex production line, say a launch vehicle. There are several
degrees of mental effort, and I do mean mental, not physical. Scientists
imagine, calculate, conceptualize. Engineers structure, plan, turn the concept
into a specification. Workers build by following the plans.

The flow runs one way, and each stage narrows the decision space of the next.
That is what makes the last stage cheap: it no longer has to choose.

With models and their effort levels it works exactly the same way. You do not
use a `low` effort to explore, conceptualize, or settle an open problem. You
use `xhigh` reasoning for that, and the low effort builds by following the
plan to the letter.

What do you gain? A very significant token saving, faster delivery, and better
quality. Because you do not put a scientist on the assembly line: it is not
their job, and they will improvise where you wanted execution.

## Pick the model before the effort

Before setting the dial, you need to know which model you are setting it on.
And there is a naming trap here.

At Anthropic the latest generation is not a single line. Opus 5 is the direct
successor to the Opus line. Fable 5 is a separate tier at twice the price per
token, with a different declared territory: the most demanding reasoning and
long-horizon agentic work. The migration documentation is explicit, word for
word: Fable 5 is not the default upgrade path for the Opus line, and a generic
request to move to the best model means Opus 5.

Concretely, a SWE-style benchmark does not separate the two. Those benchmarks
measure short, well-specified, single-objective tasks, scored automatically.
That is exactly the regime where Opus 5 caught up. Fable 5's claimed edge lies
elsewhere: single requests that run for tens of minutes, delegation to
long-running subagents, navigating ambiguity.

Hence a counterintuitive conclusion if you work with a PRD: writing a good PRD
absorbs the ambiguity upfront. And navigating ambiguity is precisely one of
Fable 5's named strengths. By structuring your upstream well, you remove part
of its advantage before it even starts.

Two non-functional constraints often settle the question before performance
does. Refusals first: the latest generation ships hardened cybersecurity
guardrails, and a request can be declined with an explicit refusal status
rather than an error. Fable 5 targets cybersecurity content broadly, and the
documentation acknowledges that legitimate adjacent work, security tooling
included, can trigger false positives. Opus 5 has the same guardrails, more
permissive. On authentication, encryption, or secrets-handling code, the most
capable model is therefore not necessarily the one that will agree to work.
Latency next: a model whose turns run for several minutes is excellent on an
autonomous run and painful in an interactive session.

## What effort does not control

Three traps, where the reflex is to touch the dial while the lever is
somewhere else.

**Verbosity.** On Opus 5, changing effort does not reliably shorten the
visible answer. It moves the volume of thinking, not the output length. If
answers run long, the lever is the prompt, not the setting. A short
conciseness instruction is enough to cut response length noticeably.

**Scope.** A model that adds steps you did not ask for is not doing it because
effort is too high. It does it because nothing tells it where to stop.

**Verification.** On recent models, explicitly asking for self-verification
produces over-verification, because they already do it. This inverts a classic
prompting best practice, and it surprises people: removing the instruction
improves behavior with no capability regression.

A word on delegation, because it is the mistake I made myself for a long time.
You often read that a high effort "spawns subagents to widen the scope". The
direction is right, the mechanism is more indirect: effort controls thinking
depth and token spend, and a low effort produces fewer, more consolidated tool
calls. Delegation to subagents comes from the harness and the model's own
tendency, not from the dial. On Opus 5 it is even worth watching in the other
direction: it delegates readily, to the point where an explicit cap helps.

## Your instruction files are context

This is the most underrated point, and the closest to the subject.

When people talk about context engineering, they think about the request
prompt. But in an agent session, what reaches the model also includes the
global instruction file, the loaded skills, the tool descriptions. That corpus
builds up over months and, in most cases, never gets audited, even though it
is reloaded on every call.

The problem is not its size. The problem is that it holds instructions written
for earlier models, which are now actively harmful. Three families come up all
the time.

**Inflationary pressure.** The capitals, the CRITICAL, the MUST, the "when in
doubt use this tool" were added because older models under-triggered. Current
models follow the system prompt very closely, so those amplifiers now cause
over-triggering.

**Scaffolding replaced by a feature.** The "think step by step", the scratchpad
tags, the response prefills to force JSON: all of it is either native now or
replaced by an API option.

**Over-specification.** A numbered procedure for a judgment task constrains the
model's exploration and degrades the output. The migration documentation says
it bluntly: prompts and skills written for earlier models are often too
prescriptive and reduce output quality.

The dividing line is simple. What only the author knows stays: the audience,
the environment, the quality bar, the constraints and above all their reasons.
What the model already knows goes: the generic virtues, the reminders of
behavior it has by default, the workarounds for defects that no longer exist.

Watch the reverse trap, the most dangerous one when you apply this
mechanically: context is not what you cut. A prompt that is too short produces
generic output, because the model fills the gaps with safe defaults.

Two notes on this table.

Review stays at `high` and no higher, because bug detection holds up at
reduced effort on Opus 5. That makes a quick pass at writing time practical,
then a deeper pass later. Raising effort here buys little.

The security audit stays on Opus 5 for a precise reason: Fable 5's
bug-detection gains are documented with an explicit exclusion of
security-focused analysis, where the cyber classifiers apply.

I run one epic per session, with a fresh context every time. Recent models
lose long-horizon coherence when constraints arrive progressively: the same
work, given at once or spread over six exchanges, does not produce the same
result, at identical model and effort.

## Every time a model ships

Do not judge a new model on its benchmarks. Those benchmarks test isolated,
well-specified cases, and vendors optimize for those scores, against their own
previous generation and against the competition. A higher number says nothing
about your codebase, your tooling and the way you work.

Do your own research. Read the vendor's posts in full rather than the summary
table: the behavioral changes, the removed parameters and the recommended
settings are in the prose. Block a day for it on every release.

Then go back over your configuration. Do your skills follow the new rules,
does your `.claude` or your `.codex` match the announced direction, do the
instructions you carry around still serve a purpose. Understand the model: its
cost, the way it works, what it is built for.
